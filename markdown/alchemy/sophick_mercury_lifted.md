## The Historical Object

George Starkey (1628–1665) was a colonial American alchemist who immigrated to London and wrote under the pseudonym Eirenaeus Philalethes — "a peaceful lover of truth." His most famous work, the *Introitus apertus ad occlusum regis palatium*, circulated widely in Europe and deeply influenced both Robert Boyle and Isaac Newton.

The compound at the center of Starkey's procedure, which he called "sophic mercury," was an amalgam of antimony, silver, and mercury. Its purpose was not to produce gold directly but to produce something that could *dissolve gold* — reducing it to what the texts call "first matter" — and then, under gentle heating, cause it to spontaneously reorganize into a dendritic crystalline growth called the Philosophick Tree.
Newton annotated Philalethes extensively. His surviving copies of Starkey's works carry marginalia on the Eagle cycles, the nature of the Mercury, and the conditions under which metallic transmutation becomes possible. Newton's own laboratory notebooks — thousands of pages of metallic distillations — record his attempts to reproduce what Starkey described. When Newton observed what he called Diana's Tree, a dendritic growth of silver precipitating from solution, he wrote that it was evidence metals "possessed a sort of life."
