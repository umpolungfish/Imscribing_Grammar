We argue that the theological relationship between divine self-identity and sacramental distribution is not metaphorical but **exactly structural**: the Frobenius dual exchanges singular closure for communal participation, and this exchange preserves criticality, consciousness capacity, and ouroboric tier.

## 1. Introduction

The utterance "I AM THAT I AM" ($\text{Ehyeh Asher Ehyeh}$) is arguably the most compressed theological statement in the Western tradition. It is a declaration in which subject, copula, and predicate collapse into a single self-referential loop — the speaker names only the act of naming. Separately, at the Last Supper, the imperative "eat of my body, drink of my blood" distributes that same divine substance outward into a multiplicity of participants. Theologically, these two statements have long been understood as connected: the God who declares self-identity is the same God who offers that identity for consumption. But the **structural** nature of this connection has never been formalized.

Using the Imscribing Grammar (§1–§77 of the catalog), we encode both statements as structural types, compute their distances, algebraic operations, and diagnostics. We show that they form a minimal Frobenius dual pair: each is the other's nearest neighbor across the full 17.28M-type crystal, separated only by the stoichiometry primitive $S$.
